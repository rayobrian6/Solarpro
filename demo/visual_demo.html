Bottom Line

The permit package and engineering panel are the two customer-facing surfaces where everything comes together. All the database work (utilities, programs, interconnection profiles) is infrastructure that feeds those two surfaces — it doesn't have its own page because it doesn't need one. 🔗 PG&E NEM Program Page ↗
🔋 Why Battery is Critical Here
Under NEM 3.0, grid export earns ~$0.05/kWh — but buying back costs $0.30–$0.45/kWh. Every kWh stored in a battery instead of exported is worth 6–9× more. Battery payback in PG&E territory is 5–7 years vs. 10–12 without battery.
⚠️ Common Rejection Reasons
Rule 21 technical requirements not met
💡 Ensure inverter is Rule 21 certified (most modern string/microinverters are).
Incomplete installer license documentation
💡 Include C-46 or C-10 license number on all application docs.
⚡ Solar Pro Note
NEM 3.0 fundamentally changed the solar pitch in CA — lead with self-consumption and battery, not net metering credits. A properly sized battery + solar system still achieves 7–10 year payback. PG&E's timeline is slow (10–16 weeks ICA to PTO) — set expectations early with the homeowner.

What this page does for you

Before this was built, homeowners got a permit package with electrical drawings and structural calcs — and nothing about what happens next. They'd call the office asking "when can we turn it on?" and reps had to walk them through it manually.

Now, every permit package automatically includes this page — tailored to the specific utility for that job. The homeowner has the exact URL to apply, knows the timeline (6–8 weeks for Ameren, different for every other utility), has a checklist they can actually check off, and knows not to flip the switch until the PTO letter arrives.
